Methodology & Formula

When fuel prices rise, you face a choice: absorb the cost (drive the same, spend more) or reduce consumption (drive less, spend the same). This tool models both outcomes from your current monthly spend and the size of the price hike.

Absorb — Keep Driving the Same

New Cost = Spend × (1 + Hike%)  •  Extra = Spend × Hike%

Cut Driving — Keep Budget Flat

New Cost = Spend  •  Driving Cut ≈ Hike%

  • Monthly spend = your current fuel budget — the baseline that gets shocked
  • Price hike = the percentage increase in pump price
  • If you hold consumption constant, cost rises one-for-one with the price
  • To keep spending unchanged, you must cut consumption — roughly by the same percentage as the price hike (exactly hike ÷ (100 + hike) × 100%)

Fuel Demand Is Inelastic in the Short Run

Most people can't immediately drive much less — commutes, deliveries, and logistics are fixed in the short term. That's why price hikes hit budgets hard before behavior adjusts. Over months, though, route optimization, carpooling, and efficiency measures can claw back a meaningful share of the increase.

Reference: Consumer Budget Elasticity Shock Model

This model applies short-run budget-elasticity reasoning to fuel price shocks: consumption is relatively inelastic immediately after a price change, so cost passes through nearly fully, with quantity adjustment growing over time. Use it for household budgeting, fleet cost forecasting, and scenario planning.
