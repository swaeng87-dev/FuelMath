Methodology & Formula

Blending ethanol into gasoline changes two key properties in opposite directions: octane rises (ethanol resists knock) while energy density falls (ethanol contains less energy per litre). This tool models both effects using volume-weighted linear blending.

Blended Octane (Linear Volume Mix)

Octaneblend = Octanebase × (1 − x) + Octaneethanol × x

Blended Energy Density

Eblend = 34.2 × (1 − x) + 23.5 × x  [MJ/L]

Fuel Economy Change

ΔEconomy = (Eblend / 34.2 − 1) × 100%

  • x = Ethanol volume fraction (e.g., 0.10 for E10)
  • 34.2 MJ/L = Gasoline energy density; 23.5 MJ/L = ethanol energy density
  • Ethanol carries ~31% less energy per litre than gasoline, so economy drops roughly in proportion to blend fraction
  • Rule of thumb: E10 ≈ −3% economy, E20 ≈ −7%, E85 ≈ −27%

Beyond Energy: Material & Water Considerations

Ethanol is hygroscopic (absorbs water), which can cause phase separation in storage, and it can degrade older rubber/elastomer fuel-system components. High blends also run cooler and may require richer calibration. These factors aren't captured in the energy math but matter for real-world blending and storage.

Reference Standard: ASTM D4806 / D5798

ASTM D4806 specifies denatured fuel ethanol for blending, and ASTM D5798 covers ethanol fuel blends (E50–E85) for flex-fuel vehicles. The octane and energy-density values used here reflect these specifications and standard ethanol blending practice.
