Methodology & Formula

The compressibility factor Z corrects the ideal gas law for real-gas molecular interactions. At low pressures Z ≈ 1; at high pressures intermolecular forces cause significant deviation. Z is essential for pipeline hydraulics, linepack calculations, and custody transfer metering.

Step 1 — Sutton Pseudo-Critical Properties

Tpc = 169.2 + 349.5γg − 74.0γg² (°R)

Ppc = 756.8 − 131.0γg − 3.75γg² (psia)

Step 2 — Reduced Properties

Tr = T / Tpc   •   Pr = P / Ppc

Step 3 — Dranchuk–Abu–Kassem EOS (11 coefficients)

Z = 1 + (A₁ + A₂/Tr + A₃/Tr³ + A₄/Tr⁴ + A₅/Tr⁵)ρr + (A₆ + A₇/Tr + A₈/Tr²)ρr² − A₉(A₇/Tr + A₈/Tr²)ρr⁵ + A₁₀(1 + A₁₁ρr²)(ρr²/Tr³)e−A₁₁ρr²

where ρr = 0.27 × Pr / (Z × Tr)

  • DAK coefficients: A₁ = 0.3265, A₂ = −1.0700, A₃ = −0.5339, A₄ = 0.01569, A₅ = −0.05165, A₆ = 0.5475, A₇ = −0.7361, A₈ = 0.1844, A₉ = 0.1056, A₁₀ = 0.6134, A₁₁ = 0.7210

Real Gas Compressibility Factor (Z-Factor)

The real gas compressibility factor, also known as the Z-factor, is a dimensionless quantity used to describe how much a real gas deviates from ideal-gas behaviour.

The compressibility factor provides a correction to the ideal gas equation. So, it is widely used in natural gas, petroleum, chemical, pipeline, CNG, and other gas-engineering calculations.

Technical Definition

It is commonly defined as:

Z = PV / nRT

where:

  • Z = compressibility factor
  • P = absolute pressure
  • V = gas volume
  • n = amount of gas in moles
  • R = universal gas constant
  • T = absolute temperature

For an ideal gas, the compressibility factor is exactly:

Z = 1

For a real gas, the value may be less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1. Everything depends on pressure, temperature, and gas composition.

Importance of the Z-Factor

The ideal gas law is useful for many applications. But it may become inaccurate when gas is compressed or when molecular interactions become significant. The real-gas equation is commonly written as:

PV = ZnRT

So the Z-factor acts as a correction factor. It shows the deviation of a real gas from ideal behaviour.

Ideal Gas and Real Gas

An ideal gas is a theoretical model based on simplifying assumptions. We assume that:

  • Gas molecules occupy negligible volume.
  • There are no intermolecular forces.
  • Collisions are perfectly elastic.
  • The gas follows the ideal gas law exactly.

Real gases do not completely satisfy these assumptions. They deviate significantly when pressure increases and as the gas approaches conditions where molecular interactions are important. So we can simplify it by saying:

Gas behavior Compressibility factor
Ideal-gas behaviourZ = 1
Negative deviation from ideal behaviorZ < 1
Positive deviation from ideal behaviorZ > 1

Methods of Z Calculation

There are several methods to calculate the compressibility factor. The appropriate method depends on the gas composition, pressure, temperature, required accuracy, and engineering application. Four common methods include:

Standing-Katz Compressibility Chart

The Standing-Katz chart is a classic industry reference for estimating the compressibility factor of natural gases. The chart relates Z to reduced pressure and temperature. It has been widely used in petroleum and natural-gas engineering.

Dranchuk-Abou-Kassem (DAK) Correlation

Please check the "Methodology and Formula" section above for the DAK correlation details used in this calculator.

Hall-Yarborough Correlation

The Hall-Yarborough method is another commonly used correlation for calculating natural-gas compressibility from reduced temperature and pressure. Like DAK, it is based on the Standing-Katz data and is commonly used in petroleum and natural-gas engineering calculations.

Equations of State

For more general thermodynamic calculations, an equation of state (EOS) is used to describe the pressure-volume-temperature relationship of a real fluid. Common cubic equations of state include:

  • Peng-Robinson (PR)
  • Soave-Redlich-Kwong (SRK)
  • Redlich-Kwong (RK)

Equations of state are particularly useful for gas mixtures and hydrocarbons when we have to consider phase behaviour, composition, and high-pressure conditions.

Reduced Pressure and Temperature

Reduced properties are used in many gas compressibility correlations rather than pressure and temperature alone.

Reduced pressure is defined as:

Pr = P / Pc

Reduced temperature is defined as:

Tr = T / Tc

where:

  • Pr = reduced pressure
  • Tr = reduced temperature
  • P = absolute pressure
  • T = absolute temperature
  • Pc = critical pressure
  • Tc = critical temperature

These reduced properties are used in generalised compressibility charts and several empirical correlations to estimate Z.

Kay's Rule

Natural gas is normally a mixture of several hydrocarbons and non-hydrocarbon components rather than a pure gas. Therefore, using the properties of a single component such as Methane may not be appropriate for the whole mixture.

A commonly used approximation for estimating the pseudocritical properties of a gas mixture is Kay's mixing rule.

The pseudocritical temperature can be estimated as:

Tpc = Σ(yi Tci)

The pseudocritical pressure can be estimated as:

Ppc = Σ(yi Pci)

where:

  • yi = mole fraction of component i
  • Tci = critical temperature of component i
  • Pci = critical pressure of component i

These pseudocritical properties can then be used to calculate approximate pseudoreduced properties:

Tpr = T / Tpc   •   Ppr = P / Ppc

The resulting pseudoreduced properties can be used with suitable compressibility correlations.

Kay's rule is an approximation and may require additional corrections when natural gas contains significant quantities of non-hydrocarbon components or under conditions where greater accuracy is required.

Impurity Corrections

Standard general compressibility correlations are suitable for gases within the composition ranges for which the underlying correlation was developed.

Natural gas may contain significant quantities of non-hydrocarbon components such as carbon dioxide (CO2) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S). These components significantly influence gas compressibility and pseudocritical properties.

For sour gases and gas mixtures containing high concentrations of CO2 or H2S, corrections may be required before using generalised compressibility correlations.

Wichert-Aziz Correction

The Wichert-Aziz correction is a very common method for considering the effects of hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide on pseudocritical properties.

It modifies the pseudocritical temperature and pressure before calculating pseudoreduced properties and subsequently calculating the compressibility factor.

This correction is particularly relevant when sour-gas compositions contain high concentrations of H2S and/or CO2. You should always check the applicable composition and operating limits of the selected correlation before relying on the result.

Critical Compressibility Factor (Zc)

The compressibility factor at the critical point is known as the critical compressibility factor, represented by Zc.

At the critical point:

P = Pc   and   T = Tc

A common misconception is that Z must equal 1 at the critical point. This is not true. Real gases generally have a critical compressibility factor different from 1.

For many simple substances, Zc is roughly in the range of 0.27 to 0.29. However, the actual value depends on the substance.

This shows an important distinction between the ideal-gas compressibility factor and the actual thermodynamic behaviour of a real fluid.

Boyle Temperature

The Boyle temperature is the temperature at which the effects of attractive and repulsive intermolecular interactions balance in the low-pressure limit.

At the Boyle temperature, the first-order deviation from ideal-gas behaviour becomes zero. Consequently, the gas can exhibit behaviour close to ideal over an appropriate low-pressure range.

The Boyle temperature should not be interpreted as a condition in which the gas is exactly ideal at every pressure. At higher pressures, additional real-gas effects can still cause Z to differ from 1.

Factors Affecting Z-Factor

Pressure

Pressure has a major influence on gas compressibility. At very low pressure, many gases approach ideal-gas behaviour and Z approaches 1. As pressure increases, molecular interactions become increasingly important.

Temperature

Temperature changes the balance between molecular kinetic energy and intermolecular forces. So, changing the temperature can significantly alter the Z-factor at a given pressure.

Gas Composition

The composition of a gas mixture affects its compressibility. Natural gas commonly contains methane along with ethane, propane, butanes, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide. Therefore, a Z-factor calculated for pure methane should not be used as the Z-factor of every natural-gas mixture.

Critical Properties

Critical temperature and pressure are important parameters in many compressibility correlations. For gas mixtures, pseudocritical properties should be used instead.

Z Factor Applications

Natural Gas

Z-factor calculations are commonly used for natural-gas volume, density, mass, pipeline flow, gas storage, and inventory calculations.

CNG Systems

Compressed natural gas is stored at high pressure and makes real-gas behaviour very important. Z-factor calculations are used in CNG storage, filling, gas quantity, pressure-volume, and density calculations.

Gas Pipelines

Pipeline engineering requires relationships between pressure, temperature, gas density, and flow. The compressibility factor is used in many gas-flow calculations.

Gas Storage

Gas storage systems operate over a wide range of pressures and temperatures. Using the appropriate Z-factor helps improve calculations of the quantity and density of stored gas.

Gas Metering

Corrections for pressure, temperature, and gas compressibility are required in gas measurement and custody-transfer calculations. The compressibility method depends on the gas composition, operating conditions, and measurement standard.

Gas Density Calculation

For a gas with molecular weight M, real-gas density can be expressed as:

ρ = PM / ZRT

where:

  • ρ = gas density
  • P = absolute pressure
  • M = molecular weight
  • Z = compressibility factor
  • R = appropriate gas constant
  • T = absolute temperature

This shows how important the Z-factor is when calculating natural-gas and other gas densities.

Gas Volume

The real-gas equation:

PV = ZnRT

can be rearranged to calculate volume:

V = ZnRT / P

We can use this equation to estimate the volume that a known quantity of gas occupies at a specified pressure and temperature.

Absolute Pressure and Temperature

Real-gas calculations with the gas law require thermodynamically consistent quantities.

In particular:

  • Use absolute pressure, not gauge pressure.
  • Use absolute temperature rather than Celsius or Fahrenheit directly.

Gauge pressure must be converted to absolute pressure before using it in gas-law calculations. Similarly, Celsius and Fahrenheit temperatures must be converted to an absolute temperature scale such as Kelvin or Rankine when needed.

Mixing incompatible units is another common source of errors in gas calculations.

Accuracy Level in Z-Factor

The accuracy of a calculated Z-factor depends on the calculation method, gas composition, input data, and operating conditions.

Different correlations and equations of state can produce different results, especially near:

  • The critical region
  • Very high pressures
  • Phase boundaries
  • Complex gas compositions
  • Conditions outside the correlation's validated range

For high-accuracy engineering work, the selected correlation or equation of state should be appropriate for the gas and the expected operating range.

Limitations

A Z-factor calculator provides an estimate based on a particular method or correlation. It does not eliminate the need to evaluate whether that method is suitable for the intended engineering application.

The result can depend on:

  • The selected correlation or equation of state
  • Gas composition
  • Pressure
  • Temperature
  • Critical or pseudocritical properties
  • Presence of CO2, H2S, or other impurities
  • Validity range of the selected method

For engineering applications, always verify that the selected calculation method, gas composition, pressure range, temperature range, units, and applicable standards are appropriate for the intended use.
