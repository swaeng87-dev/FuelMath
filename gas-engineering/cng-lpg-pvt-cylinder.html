Methodology & Formula

The Real Gas Law extends the ideal gas equation with a compressibility correction factor Z, accounting for intermolecular forces at high cylinder pressures. For CNG at 200 bar, Z ≈ 0.85–0.95; ignoring this correction overestimates mass by 10–15%.

m = (P × V × M) / (Z × R × T)
  • m = Gas mass (kg)
  • P = Absolute pressure (Pa) — gauge reading × 100,000
  • V = Water capacity (m³) — liters ÷ 1,000
  • M = Molar mass (kg/mol) — g/mol ÷ 1,000
  • Z = Compressibility factor (CNG ≈ 0.9 at 200 bar, LPG vapor ≈ 0.95)
  • R = Universal gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K)
  • T = Absolute temperature (K) = °C + 273.15

Model Limitation

LPG cylinders contain liquid in equilibrium with vapor — this PVT model applies only to the vapor space or fully vaporized contents. For liquid LPG mass, use the liquid density directly (propane ≈ 0.493 kg/L at 25°C, butane ≈ 0.573 kg/L). This calculator is accurate for CNG and gaseous-phase LPG calculations.

Reference Standard: Real Gas Law (PVT)

The real gas law is the foundational thermodynamic equation for compressed gas inventory management, cylinder custody verification, and vehicle fuel state-of-charge estimation. For precise Z values at specific pressures, use the Z-Factor Calculator.

How Does a CNG Cylinder Fill Estimator Work?

A CNG cylinder stores natural gas at high pressure. When you want to estimate how much gas is inside the cylinder, the pressure reading alone is not enough. The cylinder's internal volume, gas temperature, gas composition, and the compressibility of the gas also affect the result.

This CNG/LPG Cylinder Fill Estimator takes all these parameters into account and estimates the amount of gas inside a tank in kilograms (kg). It is very useful for understanding the relationship between CNG cylinder pressure, cylinder capacity, temperature, and gas mass.

The basic principle of the Estimator comes from the gas law. For an ideal gas, the relationship between pressure, volume, temperature, and the quantity of gas is described by:

PV = nRT

However, CNG is stored at relatively high pressure, so it does not behave exactly the same as an ideal gas. Therefore, a compressibility factor (Z) is used to differentiate between ideal and real-gas behaviour.

The real-gas equation used for estimating gas mass is:

m = (P × V × M) / (Z × R × T)
  • m = mass of the gas in kg
  • P = absolute pressure in Pa
  • V = cylinder volume in m³
  • M = molar mass of the gas in kg/mol
  • Z = compressibility factor
  • R = universal gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)
  • T = absolute temperature in Kelvin

Why Is Cylinder Water Capacity Important?

Gas cylinders are commonly identified by their water capacity, which is the internal volume of the cylinder expressed in litres. For example, a cylinder may have a water capacity of:

  • 40 L, 50 L, 60 L, 70 L, 80 L, or 100 L

A 60-litre cylinder does not contain 60 kg of CNG. The 60 L figure refers to the internal volume of the cylinder, not the mass of gas. If you filled it with water instead of CNG, it would hold exactly 60 litres. The cylinder's water capacity is generally stamped on the shoulder of the cylinder.

How Do You Calculate CNG kg from Pressure?

To estimate the CNG mass, you need four main pieces of information:

  1. Cylinder pressure
  2. Cylinder water capacity
  3. Gas temperature
  4. Gas properties (molar mass and compressibility factor)

For methane, which is the principal component of natural gas, the molar mass is approximately 16.04 g/mol. The pressure must be converted to absolute pressure before it is used in the gas-law calculation. If the pressure is given as gauge pressure:

Pabsolute = Pgauge + Patmospheric

For example, a pressure of 200 bar(g) is approximately 201 bar absolute under normal atmospheric conditions. The difference is relatively small at high CNG pressures, but it is important to distinguish between gauge and absolute pressure in a thermodynamic calculation.

  • First, the cylinder volume is converted from litres to cubic meters: 60 L = 0.060 m³
  • The temperature is then converted from Celsius to Kelvin: 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K
  • And the gauge pressure is converted to absolute pressure by adding atmospheric pressure.

These values are then substituted into the real-gas equation:

m = (P × V × M) / (Z × R × T)

The result shows the gas mass in kilograms (KG). The actual result may slightly vary depending on gas composition, temperature, and Z-factor.

Effect of Temperature on CNG Cylinder Pressure

Temperature is an important factor when considering CNG cylinder pressure. When compressed gas becomes warmer, its pressure increases. When the gas cools, its pressure decreases.

For example, a vehicle's CNG cylinder filled in cool conditions may show a different pressure after the vehicle is parked in direct sunlight. This does not necessarily mean that additional gas has entered the cylinder.

Similarly, pressure can fall after filling as the gas and cylinder temperature return to ambient conditions.

What Is the CNG Z-Factor?

The compressibility factor, represented by Z, shows that real gases do not behave exactly like ideal gases, particularly at high pressures.

For an ideal gas: Z = 1. For a real gas, the value differs from 1.

CNG is stored at high pressure, so using an appropriate Z-factor provides a much more accurate result than assuming ideal-gas behaviour. The appropriate Z-factor depends on factors such as:

  • Gas composition
  • Pressure
  • Temperature

Therefore, there is no single Z-factor that is correct for every CNG cylinder and every operating condition.

CNG Cylinder Capacity and Gas Mass

How much CNG can be stored depends strongly on cylinder volume and filling pressure. As a general principle:

Higher cylinder volume → more gas at the same pressure
Higher pressure → more gas in the same cylinder

However, the relationship is not always perfectly linear because real-gas behaviour and temperature affect the result. For example, a 100 L cylinder at a particular pressure will generally contain more CNG than a 50 L cylinder at the same pressure, assuming gas composition, temperature, and Z-factor remain identical.

Why 200 Bar Does Not Always Mean the Same Amount of CNG

A pressure reading such as 200 bar is not a fixed measurement of CNG mass. The amount of gas corresponding to 200 bar depends on the cylinder volume, temperature, molar mass, and Z-factor.

For example, 200 bar in a small cylinder and 200 bar in a much larger cylinder represent different gas volumes. The larger the cylinder, the higher the amount of gas inside it.

Temperature can also drastically change the relationship between pressure and mass. A 60 L cylinder at 200 bar at 20°C results in 8.774 kg of CNG. But when the temperature is increased to 30°C, the amount of CNG reduces to 8.485 kg.

Similarly, molar mass and Z-factor play an important role. When the composition of the gas changes, the gas mass changes even if all other parameters remain the same.

Therefore, statements such as "200 bar equals X kg of CNG" are incomplete unless the cylinder volume, temperature, and gas properties are also specified.

CNG vs LPG: An Important Difference

CNG and LPG should not be treated as identical when estimating cylinder contents.

CNG

CNG is primarily stored as a compressed gas. A pressure-volume-temperature (PVT) calculation is therefore highly useful for calculating the gas mass inside a CNG cylinder.

LPG

LPG is normally stored in a cylinder primarily as a liquid, with a vapour space above the liquid. Because of this, cylinder pressure does not directly indicate the total mass of LPG in the cylinder. The PVT calculation on this page is intended strictly for the gaseous phase of LPG.

Liquid LPG Mass Calculation

When LPG is in liquid form, mass must be estimated using density rather than the gas law:

m = ρ × V
  • m = liquid LPG mass
  • ρ = liquid LPG density
  • V = liquid volume

LPG density varies with composition and temperature. Propane, butane, and commercial LPG mixtures do not share the same densities. This is why a domestic LPG cylinder's rated filling quantity, such as 14.2 kg, should not be calculated simply from the cylinder's pressure.

Factors That Affect the Estimated Gas Mass

  • Cylinder Volume: A larger cylinder can hold more gas at the same pressure and temperature.
  • Pressure: Increasing pressure generally increases the amount of compressed gas in the cylinder.
  • Temperature: Gas temperature affects pressure, and therefore affects the relationship between pressure and mass.
  • Gas Composition: Natural gas is not always pure methane. It can contain other hydrocarbons and inert gases. The composition affects the gas's molar mass and thermodynamic properties.
  • Compressibility Factor: The Z-factor accounts for real-gas behaviour and can vary with pressure, temperature, and gas composition.
  • Measurement Accuracy: Pressure gauges have limited accuracy. A small pressure-reading error can affect the estimated mass.
  • Cylinder Specifications: The stated water capacity is a nominal cylinder specification. Actual manufactured dimensions and tolerances can affect the precise internal volume.
